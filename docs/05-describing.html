5 Describing Data

In Term 1, you used R to describe data and even ran a t-test. Over the next three sessions, we will go back over some of this ground, but more slowly and with a clear purpose: to understand what the numbers mean, how sure we can be about them, and when a difference between groups is real. We will use one question throughout: who breathes the most polluted air in London? This week, we will set up a tidy workflow in R and use it to describe our data. This session and the next link to the first statistics worksheet.

ImportantBefore the session

Make sure R and RStudio are working on your laptop. You installed both in Term 1; if you run into problems, you can use Desktop@UCL Anywhere instead. Then run the following once in the RStudio console to install the tidyverse:

install.packages('tidyverse')

5.1 Air pollution in London

Nitrogen dioxide (NO2) mostly comes from road traffic, and long-term exposure is linked to asthma and other respiratory diseases. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that the annual mean should not exceed 10 µg/m³, with interim targets of 40, 30 and 20 µg/m³ for places that are still far above this guideline. The UK legal limit is 40 µg/m³. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) comes from traffic, wood burning and many other sources, and has a WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³.

Air pollution is a physical phenomenon, but who is exposed to it is a social question. Researchers refer to this as environmental justice: are poorer neighbourhoods exposed to more pollution than richer ones? Over the next three sessions, we will describe, estimate and test exactly that.

5.2 The data

We will be using a dataset that contains one row for each of London’s 4,994 Lower Super Output Areas (LSOAs): small statistical areas with, on average, about 1,500 residents. For each LSOA, the dataset contains the following variables:

Column Description
lsoa_code, lsoa_name LSOA code and name (2021)
borough, inner_outer London borough, and whether it is in Inner or Outer London
population Residents (mid-2022)
no2, pm25 Modelled annual mean NO2 and PM2.5, 2022 (µg/m³), averaged over the LSOA
imd_score, imd_decile Index of Multiple Deprivation 2025: score, and decile within England (1 = most deprived 10%)
income_score Share of residents who are income-deprived (0 to 1)

The NO2 and PM2.5 values are averages over whole LSOAs. No LSOA as a whole is above the UK legal limit, but many roadside locations within them are.

NoteData sources

Greater London Authority, London Atmospheric Emissions Inventory (LAEI) 2022, 20 m concentration grids, averaged per LSOA by GEOG0187 staff. Ministry of Housing, Communities & Local Government, English Indices of Deprivation 2025. LSOA boundaries: Office for National Statistics. All under the Open Government Licence v3.0.

5.4 A tidy workflow

In Term 1, you worked with base R: functions such as read.csv() and tapply(), and the $ operator. In these sessions, we will switch to the tidyverse: a collection of packages that share a common way of working. You take a dataset and pass it through a series of small steps using the pipe, |>, which you can read as “and then”. This is also how R is used in most Year 2 methods modules.

Let us start by loading the tidyverse and reading in the data. The glimpse() function gives us a first look at the dataset: the number of rows and columns, the type of each column, and the first few values.

R code
# load the tidyverse
library(tidyverse)

# read the data
lsoa <- read_csv('data/london-lsoa-air-quality.csv')

The tidyverse is built around a small number of verbs, each of which does one thing. The table below lists the most important ones, together with their base R equivalents from Term 1:

Verb What it does Base R from Term 1
select() keep some columns df[, c('a', 'b')]
filter() keep rows that meet a condition df[df$a > 10, ]
mutate() add or change a column df$c <- df$a / df$b
arrange() sort rows df[order(df$a), ]
group_by() + summarise() summarise per group tapply(df$a, df$g, mean)
count() count rows per group table(df$g)

We can use mutate() to add new columns. Every LSOA in London is above the WHO guideline of 10 µg/m³, so instead we will flag each LSOA that is above the WHO interim target of 20 µg/m³. We will also divide London’s LSOAs into five equally sized groups (quintiles) based on income deprivation, from 1 (the least deprived fifth) to 5 (the most deprived fifth). Because we will use both new columns again, we save the result by assigning it back to lsoa:

R code
# add two columns
lsoa <- lsoa |>
  mutate(above_20 = no2 > 20,
         income_quintile = ntile(income_score, 5))

# check: about 1,000 LSOAs in each quintile
lsoa |>
  count(income_quintile)
# A tibble: 5 × 2
  income_quintile     n
            <int> <int>
1               1   999
2               2   999
3               3   999
4               4   999
5               5   998

Why do we use income deprivation rather than the overall Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD)? The IMD includes a Living Environment domain that partly measures air quality, so comparing NO2 with the IMD would partly mean comparing air pollution with itself. Income deprivation avoids this problem.

5.5 Describing a single variable

When we describe a numerical variable, we usually report three things: its centre, its spread and its shape. We can calculate the most common summary statistics in one go with summarise():

R code
# centre and spread of NO2
lsoa |>
  summarise(n = n(),
            mean = mean(no2),
            median = median(no2),
            sd = sd(no2),
            iqr = IQR(no2),
            min = min(no2),
            max = max(no2))
# A tibble: 1 × 7
      n  mean median    sd   iqr   min   max
  <int> <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl> <dbl> <dbl> <dbl>
1  4994  21.7   21.4  3.27   4.3  13.1  36.3
  • Centre: the mean is the average, and the median is the middle value. If the two differ a lot, the distribution is skewed or contains outliers.
  • Spread: the standard deviation (sd) is roughly the typical distance of a value from the mean, and the interquartile range (IQR) is the range covered by the middle 50% of values.
  • Shape: is the distribution symmetric or skewed, and does it have one peak or more? To see this, we need a plot.

For plotting, we will keep using base R, as in Term 1. Note that the $ operator still works on a dataset loaded with the tidyverse:

R code
# histogram, with the WHO interim target as a dashed line
hist(lsoa$no2, breaks = 40,
     main = 'NO2 in London LSOAs, 2022',
     xlab = 'Annual mean NO2 (µg/m³)', ylab = 'Number of LSOAs',
     col = 'grey80', border = 'white')
abline(v = 20, lty = 2)

Proportions are means too. Because above_20 is either TRUE (1) or FALSE (0), its mean is the share of LSOAs above the interim target. Try running mean(lsoa$above_20). What does the answer tell you?

5.5.1 Areas or people?

The mean we calculated treats every LSOA as one unit. However, LSOAs differ in the number of people who live in them, and if we want to say something about people, we should count people. A population-weighted mean does exactly that:

R code
# average LSOA vs average Londoner
lsoa |>
  summarise(mean_lsoa = mean(no2),
            mean_resident = weighted.mean(no2, population))
# A tibble: 1 × 2
  mean_lsoa mean_resident
      <dbl>         <dbl>
1      21.7          21.8

In this case the two values are very close, but it is always worth asking yourself whether your unit of analysis is the area or the person.

5.6 Describing groups

We can compare groups by combining group_by() with summarise(), the tidyverse equivalent of tapply(). Let us compare NO2 levels in Inner and Outer London:

R code
# NO2 in Inner and Outer London
lsoa |>
  group_by(inner_outer) |>
  summarise(n = n(),
            mean = mean(no2),
            median = median(no2),
            sd = sd(no2))
# A tibble: 2 × 5
  inner_outer     n  mean median    sd
  <chr>       <int> <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl>
1 Inner        1966  24.4   24.2  2.66
2 Outer        3028  20.0   20    2.31

A boxplot shows the median, the IQR (the box) and any outliers for each group side by side. The formula no2 ~ inner_outer can be read as “NO2 by Inner or Outer London”:

R code
# boxplot by group
boxplot(no2 ~ inner_outer, data = lsoa,
        main = 'NO2 by part of London',
        xlab = '', ylab = 'Annual mean NO2 (µg/m³)')

The same approach works for the boroughs. With 33 boroughs, it helps to sort the result, so that we can look at the boroughs with the highest and the lowest levels:

R code
# borough summary, sorted by median NO2
borough_no2 <- lsoa |>
  group_by(borough) |>
  summarise(n = n(),
            median = median(no2),
            iqr = IQR(no2),
            share_above_20 = mean(above_20)) |>
  arrange(desc(median))

5.7 Pollution and deprivation

Let us now return to our environmental justice question, and look at NO2 levels for each income deprivation quintile:

R code
# NO2 by income deprivation quintile
lsoa |>
  group_by(income_quintile) |>
  summarise(n = n(),
            mean = mean(no2),
            median = median(no2),
            sd = sd(no2))
# A tibble: 5 × 5
  income_quintile     n  mean median    sd
            <int> <int> <dbl>  <dbl> <dbl>
1               1   999  20.9   20.3  3.69
2               2   999  21.4   21.2  3.24
3               3   999  21.9   21.4  3.18
4               4   999  22.0   21.5  2.96
5               5   998  22.5   22.1  3.00

And plot the result:

R code
boxplot(no2 ~ income_quintile, data = lsoa,
        main = 'NO2 by income deprivation',
        xlab = 'Income deprivation quintile (1 = least, 5 = most deprived)',
        ylab = 'Annual mean NO2 (µg/m³)')

It is worth looking carefully before drawing any conclusions. Is the pattern the same all the way from quintile 1 to quintile 5? Also remember that both deprived areas and polluted areas are more common in Inner London. Repeat the summary within Inner and Outer London, using group_by(inner_outer, income_quintile), and see whether the pattern still holds.

Describing is not the same as explaining. A difference between groups tells us that exposure is unequal, but not why: roads, housing, history and planning all play a part.

5.8 Reporting your results

Numbers only become useful once we put them into words. In a report, an essay or a worksheet, you would describe your data in a short paragraph supported by a figure, rather than by copying R output. A good description covers:

  • what was measured, in which units, for which units of analysis (LSOAs, people), and how many;
  • the centre and spread, with suitable measures (median and IQR if the data are skewed);
  • the shape, shown in a figure that you refer to;
  • how groups differ, in numbers and in words, without claiming more than the data show.

Based on this session’s results, this could look as follows:

Across London’s 4,994 LSOAs, modelled annual mean NO2 in 2022 had a median of 21.4 µg/m³ (IQR 4.3 µg/m³), ranging from 13.1 to 36.3 µg/m³. Every LSOA was above the WHO guideline of 10 µg/m³, and 69% were above the interim target of 20 µg/m³. Levels were clearly higher in Inner London (median 24.2 µg/m³) than in Outer London (20.0 µg/m³).
